## 1. The finding that most threatens our framing

**Graphs of convex sets already contain an oracle formulation, and it is a numbered remark in the
nearest-competitor lineage.**

`2024_marcucci_shortest-paths-gcs_arXiv-2101.11565.pdf`, Remark 5.9 (arXiv v5 numbering —
**re-locate it in the published SIAM article before citing the number**):

> "each set $X_v$ may be very complex and accessible only through an oracle that, given a point
> $x_v$, either certifies that $x_v \in X_v$ or returns a separating hyperplane. In fact, such an
> oracle is easily adapted to checking membership to the perspective cones in (5.5e), and this
> black-box access to the problem constraints is sufficient for efficient optimization algorithms
> like the ellipsoid method."

The contributions section makes the same point: the relaxation is *"set based, in the sense that it
does not rely on the explicit constraints that define the sets in our GCS"*.

**Our claim survives, and the difference must be stated in the paper rather than left for a reader
to find.** It now is, as a named paragraph in `paper/journal_1/main.tex` §Related work:

1. **Different object.** Theirs is a *separation* oracle on a convex *safe set*, answering
   membership. Ours is a *support* oracle on the *hazard*: a nearest point, and a rigorous bound on
   how far the set reaches in one direction.
2. **Different consequence.** Their remark removes the inequality description of a region that has
   already been constructed. It does not remove the construction, does not bound how many regions a
   scene needs, and does not change that the graph and its adjacency come from a decomposition of
   free space. Ours removes the decomposition.
3. **Different status.** It is a theoretical observation naming only the ellipsoid method; every
   experiment in both GCS papers uses explicit convex sets with a standard conic solver.

## 2. Deits & Tedrake, IRIS — `2015_deits_iris_wafr.pdf`

Full entry, verified against Crossref `10.1007/978-3-319-16595-0_7`: *Algorithmic Foundations of
Robotics XI*, Springer Tracts in Advanced Robotics **107**, pp. 109–124, Springer, Cham, 2015
(WAFR 2014). Two authors. The archived copy is the authors' camera-ready, byte-identical to the
MIT copy; section numbers were verified against the preprint, **not** the Springer typesetting.

### What it establishes, and what it does not

| claim we made | verdict |
|---|---|
| alternates a QP for separating hyperplanes with an SDP for the inscribed ellipsoid, one region per seed | **confirmed** — but only the ellipsoid step is an SDP; do not write "two semidefinite programs" |
| requires a seed point | **confirmed** |
| seeds are placed by sampling | **refuted.** §1: *"we expect that a human operator or a higher-level planning algorithm can provide helpful guidance"*; Fig. 2 seeds are *"chosen by a human operator"*. §2 explicitly contrasts the paper with sampling-based alternatives. Sampled seeding belongs to the pipelines built on IRIS, not to IRIS |
| works above three dimensions | **confirmed** — §4, *"extends without modification to higher dimensions"* |
| cost scales with dimension | **not addressed.** Every scaling statement is in obstacle count. The 4D result is a figure with no timing |
| a collection of regions need not cover free space | **confirmed**, and presented as a deliberate trade-off, not a defect |
| computed before the optimization | **confirmed in substance**, but the downstream consumer in this paper is footstep *position* selection, not trajectory optimization — do not let this citation carry a trajectory-optimization claim |
| moving obstacles | **absent.** No notion of time anywhere in the paper |

### The part that contradicted us — verified quote by quote

The paper's selling point is that region construction is *cheap*:

- Abstract: *"linear in the number of obstacles, and our matlab implementation converges in seconds
  for environments with millions of obstacles."*
- §1: *"we can compute a convex region in an environment containing one million obstacles in just
  a few seconds."*
- §1, and this is the sharp one: *"the favorable performance of our algorithm for large numbers of
  obstacles means that the decomposition of the obstacles need not be minimal."* — i.e. complicated
  obstacle geometry becomes more convex pieces, and more pieces are nearly free.

**Our earlier sentence "free-space representation is an expense that grows with the geometry of the
obstacles" is directly contested by this paper and had to be re-aimed.** What survives, and what
the draft now says:

- The measurement is **per region, from a given seed**. Nothing in the paper bounds how many
  regions a scene needs, and §1 concedes coverage may be incomplete. The cost of a usable
  representation is per-region cost times region count, and only the first factor is measured.
- **No evidence above three dimensions.** Every number is 2D or 3D.
- Configuration-space obstacles are **assumed**, not built — §4: *"assuming that the
  $N$-dimensional configuration space obstacles can be generated."* The lifting cost we point at
  lies outside their measurement boundary.
- Convex obstacles are required; external convex decomposition is deferred to and never timed.
- Fully static, so none of it speaks to the space-time setting.

**Do not argue that IRIS is expensive in obstacle count. We would lose, against their own
measurements.** Argue region *count*, dimension, and the unmeasured lifting and decomposition
steps — and prefer Osburn's Table I, which measures the assembly in our actual setting.

## 3. Marcucci et al., graphs of convex sets — two papers, and they differ

`2024_marcucci_shortest-paths-gcs_arXiv-2101.11565.pdf` (arXiv v5) — SIAM J. Optimization
**34**(1):507–532, 2024, DOI `10.1137/22M1523790`.
`2023_marcucci_motion-planning-convex-opt_arXiv-2205.04422.pdf` (arXiv **v1 only**) — *Science
Robotics* **8**(84), article `eadf7843`, 2023, DOI `10.1126/scirobotics.adf7843`.

**Citation hazard.** The archived Science Robotics copy is the May 2022 preprint; the published
article is a condensed Science-format piece from November 2023 with **different section
numbering**. Never cite a section number of that paper without re-locating it in the published
version. (The SIAM PDF's own embedded metadata misspells the first author as "T. Maruccci" — do
not let that reach the bibliography.)

### Corrections to claims our draft made

- **"A single convex relaxation" is the Science Robotics planner, not the SIAM paper.** The SIAM
  paper solves the mixed-integer program exactly by branch and bound, and reports relaxation gaps
  up to 32.9 % with a worked instance where the relaxation *"can, in principle, be arbitrarily
  loose."*
- **"Rather than by search over discrete choices" is false.** The rounding in the Science Robotics
  planner is *"a randomized depth-first search with backtracking"* over candidate paths, followed
  by a small convex program per path. The draft now says: one large relaxation plus a cheap
  randomized search over rounded paths.
- **"A constraint must be expressible as stay-inside-this-convex-region" is too strong and would
  have been refuted in one line.** Edges carry joint convex constraints on both endpoint decision
  vectors — that is how dynamics, boundary conditions and continuity enter — and a vertex's
  decision vector is a whole stack of control points, with constraints on derivatives and timing
  that are not region containment. The correct statement, now in the draft: *any convex constraint
  on one vertex's decision vector, or jointly on the two endpoints of an edge.*
  **Their own stated limitation is the better ground for us:** optimizing trajectory shape and
  timing jointly makes continuous-time dynamics constraints nonconvex in their formulation, and
  manipulator task-space constraints are *"not directly suitable."*
- **"Needs no initial guess" is not their claim and is only half true of the pipeline.** Neither
  paper states it. The regions require seeds, *"produced manually, using inverse kinematics"* in
  the reported experiments. Region construction took **53 s** against query times of
  **0.01–0.61 s** — roughly two orders of magnitude — and their defence is amortization:
  *"the same data structure ... can be used to plan the motion of the robot for many initial and
  final conditions."* **Amortization requires the free space to be static. That is our counter,
  and it is why the moving-obstacle setting is the right place to make the argument.**
- **Bézier and the hull property belong to the Science Robotics paper only.** The SIAM paper
  contains no Bézier material. Their use is the same as ours — all control points of a segment in
  the region certifies the segment — and they flag it as sufficient rather than necessary, with
  conservatism falling as degree rises.
- **Perspective operators are the source of the relaxation's tightness**, and it is the SIAM
  paper's headline contribution. Confirmed throughout.
- **Optimality is with respect to the transcription**, which is a little weaker than "with respect
  to the graph": the graph, plus the control-point sufficient conditions, plus convex *upper
  bounds* on the cost. We may state this more sharply than we had.
- **Neither paper handles moving obstacles.** The free-space model is static by construction; time
  enters only as a monotone time-scaling along a path through static regions. Their future-work
  list does not mention moving obstacles. One speculative sentence suggests PRM techniques for
  changing environments *"can be translated to GCS with relatively low effort"* — no method, no
  result; know it exists so it is not a surprise.

### Unexpectedly in our favour

The Science Robotics formulation **separates region membership from passing time**: the vertex
constraint is purely spatial, and the time scaling is constrained only by monotonicity, velocity
and duration bounds. Their decomposition demonstrably does **not** bind the passing time. That is
independent, reviewer-side confirmation of the standing trap — never write that a decomposition
binds the passing time — and it is the converse of the sentence in the submitted KSAS manuscript.

## 4. Zhang, Yadmellat & Gao — `2021_zhang_hull-property-spatiotemporal-corridors_arXiv-2110.00065.pdf`

**Three bibliographic defects, all now fixed.** The entry read *Zhi Zhang … Ke Gao*, `@article`,
arXiv-only, 2021. Correct: **Weize Zhang, Peyman Yadmellat, Zhiwei Gao**, *2022 IEEE Intelligent
Vehicles Symposium (IV)*, pp. **1033–1039**, DOI `10.1109/IV51971.2022.9827156`, 2022 — confirmed
against the PDF title block and Crossref. It is **not** arXiv-only, which strengthens the citation.

**Our "no side condition" claim is confirmed, and the paper states our reason almost verbatim.**

- The failure is real: *"when there is a time-dependency … the convex hull property does not
  generally hold … imposing a limitation on the shape of corridors."* Fig. 2 is the explicit
  counterexample.
- Their diagnosis, and the sentence to cite: **"This is stemmed from the fact that the spatial axis
  and the temporal axis are not equivalent."**
- The condition (Theorem 2.1) constrains the **time parameterisation**, not the corridor shape or
  the curve degree: control points must sit at equally spaced times, each within the corridor
  bounds evaluated at *its own* time.
- The setting is **scalar position as a function of time** — one spatial coordinate against time,
  the Bernstein parameter *is* time, a control point is a single number. No lifting anywhere in the
  paper; "half-space", "hyperplane" and "lifted" do not occur.

**Phrase our sentence as "needs no side condition", never as "the condition holds anyway."** Our
control-point times are decision variables and generally unequally spaced, so we would *violate*
their hypothesis; containment in the lifted space simply does not need it.

**They do not pre-empt anything of ours.** Their own statement of the plain hull property is a
footnote citing a 1990 surface-intersection paper, so *"see Zhang for its use in corridors"* is a
weak citation and the draft no longer makes it. A better source for the hull property in corridors
is still to be chosen — candidates, both to be read first: Ding et al. (SSC, RA-L 2019, already
archived) and Gao et al. (ICRA 2018).

## 5. SPOT — `2026_srivastava_spot-spatiotemporal-uav-planning_arXiv-2602.01189.pdf`

Real entry, from the PDF: **Srivastava, Chackenkulam, Teja, Thomas, Krishna**, *"SPOT:
Spatio-Temporal Obstacle-free Trajectory Planning for UAVs in Unknown Dynamic Environments"*,
**accepted at ICRA 2026**, arXiv:2602.01189v3. Our record of it was second-hand and had the
acronym right and nothing else; there was no bib entry at all.

**Our sentence about it was wrong in three ways, and the corrected version supports us better.**

1. **It is not a convex-optimization method.** Minimum-jerk polynomial parameterization with an
   unconstrained penalty solve (L-BFGS), not a conic program; containment is imposed at waypoints
   with a soft violation penalty, not certified along the curve.
2. **It never builds a four-dimensional convex region.** The corridor is a sequence of ordinary
   **3D** polytopes, one per path segment, each inflated against the **union** of obstacle
   positions predicted over that segment's time window. That union is the price, and it is why the
   window must be short.
3. **The 2 s figure is a per-segment time window, not a planning horizon.** With prediction binned
   at 0.2 s, and the stated reason is *"a balance between prediction fidelity and computational
   tractability"* — both, not tractability alone.

Also: time is a coordinate in its sampler only; the optimization fixes the time allocation and
searches the spatial waypoints. **The paper reports no solver or planning time anywhere** — its
"Time (s)" column is mission completion time, which the arithmetic confirms (16.77 m / 19.13 s just
under its 1 m/s cap). **Any argument leaning on SPOT's cost measurements has nothing to stand on.**

Two cautions. It runs onboard a Jetson in real time with up to 30 dynamic obstacles, which a
hostile reader could read as "the 4D case is cheap"; the rebuttal is finding 2 — it is cheap
*because* the region stays 3D, at 1 m/s within a 6 m sensing range. And it calls its sampler
"ST-RRT\*" without citing Grothe et al., whose ST-RRT\* we already cite: two independent
constructions sharing an acronym, to be disambiguated if both appear in one paragraph.
